Threads Inside the Libraries, Step by Step

NumPy and scikit-learn start threads of their own. What happens on the CPUs when a library splits one call among threads, and when a pool of workers each starts its own threads? Measured on our cluster.

    About the measurements and the model

    Where. haskell, a node of our cluster (AMD EPYC 9755), 7 November 2026. One product: a job with 8 CPUs (4 cores), best of 3. Pools: a job with 4 CPUs (2 cores), 16 tasks, each the same 16 billion operations as 64 products of 500 × 500 matrices or 1000 products of 200 × 200.

    Measured. 4 workers × 1 thread: 1.41 s and 1.40 s. 4 workers × 4 threads: 6.0 s with 500 × 500 products and 88.5 s with 200 × 200. The CPUs stayed 99-100% busy in every run; with 4 threads per worker the processes used 24 s and 354 s of CPU time for about 5.5 s of work, almost all of it in user mode: the waiting threads spin.

    The model in views 3 and 4 has one number, a time slice of 5 ms, chosen to fit these two measurements. It assumes that the 4 parts of a product run in 4 different slices and that a thread that has finished spins until its slice ends. Real schedules are less regular, but the main effect is the same: a product takes a few slices however small it is, so small products suffer most.

    The fix. Parallelize one level and set the other to 1: 4 workers × 1 thread, or 1 process × 4 threads. joblib does this on its own: each worker gets CPUs ÷ n_jobs threads.
